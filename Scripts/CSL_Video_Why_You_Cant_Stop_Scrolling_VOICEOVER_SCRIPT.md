# CSL Video #1 — Voiceover Script (Humanised, Read-Aloud)

## "Why You Can't Stop Scrolling — Even When You're Bored of It"

**Channel:** Cognitive Strategy Lab (CSL)
**This version:** written for YOU to record in your own voice. Simple words, short lines, told like a story.
**Length:** ~1,750 spoken words → about 11–12 minutes at a calm pace.
**Companion file:** `CSL_Video_Why_You_Cant_Stop_Scrolling_PRODUCTION_PLAN.md` (the full editing/After Effects plan).
**Audience:** USA, UK, Canada, Australia. General viewers, not experts.

---

## HOW TO READ THIS ALOUD (quick guide for your voiceover)

- **Talk, don't perform.** Read it like you're explaining something interesting to one friend across a table. Not a stage. One person.
- **Pace:** slow and calm. Around 140–150 words a minute. When in doubt, slower.
- **The cues in brackets are for you:**
  - `(pause)` = stop for about 1 second. Let it breathe.
  - `(beat)` = a tiny half-second gap. A breath.
  - *italics* = lean on this word a little. Slight emphasis.
  - `(warm)` / `(quiet)` / `(firm)` = the feeling to put in your voice for that bit.
- **`[VISUAL: ...]`** lines are NOT spoken. They're notes for editing — they tell you what's on screen while you say that line. Ignore them when recording; use them when editing.
- **Read the whole thing out loud once before recording.** If a sentence is hard to say, change it. It's your voice — it should fit your mouth.

### [0:00 – 0:30] — THE HOOK

**[VISUAL: Almost black. A face lit only by the glow of a phone. Nothing moves except a thumb, scrolling.]**

You picked up your phone to check one thing.

`(beat)`

**[VISUAL: Small clock in the corner. 9:14 PM.]**

Forty minutes later, you're watching a video about a topic you've never cared about.

**[VISUAL: The clock now says 9:54 PM. The face hasn't moved. The thumb keeps going.]**

And here's the strange part. `(beat)` You're not even *enjoying* it.

`(pause)`

**[VISUAL: Slow push in on the eyes. Glassy. Unfocused.]**

You're not having fun. You're not learning anything. You'd actually like to stop.

`(beat)`

But you don't.

`(pause)`

So today I want to show you the *exact* mechanism your phone uses to keep you there. (quiet) Not willpower tips. The real reason. And once you see it, you can't un-see it.

**[VISUAL: Title card — "WHY CAN'T YOU STOP?" in gold serif on navy. Hold 1 second.]**

---

### [0:30 – 1:30] — THE PUZZLE

Let me start with a word.

**[VISUAL: A single word fades up — "brain rot."]**

In 2024, Oxford picked their Word of the Year. Out of every word in the English language, the one they chose was *brain rot*.

`(beat)`

Not "AI." Not anything political. Brain rot. (beat) That's how many of us quietly agreed that something was happening to our heads.

**[VISUAL: Quick collage of headlines — "Oxford Word of the Year 2024: Brain Rot."]**

And two years on, people are still typing the same question into Google, late at night, when they can't sleep:

**[VISUAL: A search bar. Words type themselves — "why can't I stop scrolling…"]**

*"Why can't I stop scrolling… even when I'm bored of it?"*

`(pause)`

Now sit with that last part, because it's the weird bit. (beat) Most habits make sense. You drink coffee because you *like* coffee. You eat the cake because the cake is *good*.

But scrolling? Half the time you're bored. Sometimes you're even a little annoyed. (beat) And you keep going anyway.

**[VISUAL: A face mid-scroll. A small sigh. Still scrolling.]**

That doesn't add up. Why would anyone keep doing something they're not enjoying?

`(beat)`

The answer is not that you're weak. (firm) It's that *two* different things are working on you at the same time. Two mechanisms. And both of them were *designed*.

**[VISUAL: Two icons appear — a slot machine, and a brain. Labels: "Mechanism 1" and "Mechanism 2."]**

Let me walk you through both. One explains why you *start*. The other explains why you *can't stop*.

---

### [1:30 – 4:30] — MECHANISM ONE: THE SLOT MACHINE IN YOUR POCKET

**[VISUAL: Title — "MECHANISM ONE: VARIABLE REWARDS" in gold serif.]**

Back in the 1990s, there was a researcher at Stanford named B.J. Fogg.

**[VISUAL: Simple line illustration of Stanford. Citation card: "B.J. Fogg — Stanford Persuasive Technology Lab."]**

He ran something called the Persuasive Technology Lab. (beat) And it was exactly what it sounds like — a place that studied how to use technology to change what people do.

Some of the students who passed through that world went on to help build the early hooks of the apps you use every single day.

`(beat)`

But here's what's interesting. The thing they used wasn't some new piece of code. (slow) It was *old* psychology. Stuff we've understood for decades.

And the most powerful piece of it was this — not the colours, not the buttons. (beat) It was *when* you get rewarded.

**[VISUAL: A slot machine. The lever pulls. Reels spin. Sometimes a win. Mostly nothing.]**

So let me ask you something. Think about a slot machine. (beat) Why are they so hard to walk away from?

It's not because you win a lot. You don't. (beat) It's because you *might*. You never know which pull is the one. (slow) That "you never know" is the whole trick.

`(pause)`

Scientists have a name for it. They call it a *variable reward*. And the rule goes like this:

**[VISUAL: Text builds, word by word. Key words in gold.]**

A reward you get *every* time… gets boring fast. (beat) But a reward you get *sometimes* — with no pattern, no warning — (slow) that is the most addictive setup the human brain has ever met.

`(pause)`

And that — (beat) that is *exactly* how your feed works.

**[VISUAL: The slot machine morphs into a phone. The reels become posts. The lever becomes a thumb pulling down to refresh.]**

Think about it. You open the app, and most of what you see is… nothing. Forgettable. (beat) Then a couple of posts are okay. And then — every now and then — one of them actually lands. A laugh. A bit of news. A face you know.

A little hit of *yes*.

**[VISUAL: A feed scrolling past. Most posts are grey. Every so often, one flashes gold.]**

And that mix — mostly nothing, with the *occasional* hit — is not an accident. (firm) Somebody measured that. Tuned it. Tested it on millions of people. Because that exact spacing is what keeps your thumb moving.

`(pause)`

But here's the part that really got me.

**[VISUAL: Two small peaks side by side. "Getting the reward" (small) and "Expecting the reward" (bigger).]**

The biggest hit of dopamine — that little chemical buzz — doesn't come when you *find* the good post. (slow) It comes *before*. In the wanting. In the "maybe the next one."

`(beat)`

Which means all that boring scrolling, post after post after post? (quiet) To your brain, that's not the price you pay. That's the *drug itself*.

**[VISUAL: Three icons — pull-to-refresh, autoplay, infinite scroll.]**

Pull down to refresh. Autoplay. Infinite scroll. (beat) Three of the most successful designs of the last fifteen years. (slow) And all three are slot machine moves. Pure and simple.

`(pause)`

So now you know why you *started*. (beat) But that still doesn't explain why you keep going when you're *bored*. Why stopping feels so hard, even when it's not fun anymore.

For that… we have to go somewhere a little darker.

---

### [4:30 – 7:30] — MECHANISM TWO: THE DEBT YOU CAN'T SEE

**[VISUAL: Title — "MECHANISM TWO: THE DOPAMINE DEBT" in gold serif. The screen feels heavier, dimmer.]**

There's a doctor at Stanford named Anna Lembke.

**[VISUAL: Citation card: "Anna Lembke — Stanford School of Medicine."]**

She spent her career treating addiction — alcohol, drugs, gambling, the serious stuff. (beat) And a few years ago, she started noticing the *same* patterns in people whose only problem… was their phone.

`(beat)`

Her big idea is simple. So stay with me, because once you get this picture, everything clicks.

**[VISUAL: A see-saw. "Pleasure" on one side. "Pain" on the other. Perfectly level.]**

In your brain, pleasure and pain share the same space. (slow) Picture a see-saw. Pleasure on one side. Pain on the other. And your brain has one job — (beat) keep it level.

So every time you feel a little pleasure — a like, a laugh, a video that hits — (beat) the see-saw tips toward pleasure.

**[VISUAL: The see-saw tips down on the pleasure side.]**

Good feeling. (beat) But your brain doesn't *like* being tipped. So it does something automatic. It pushes back the *other* way. It tips toward pain — by the exact same amount.

**[VISUAL: The see-saw swings back, and dips down on the pain side.]**

`(pause)`

And *that* — that little tip toward pain — is the flat, empty feeling you get right *after* the good post. (slow) The joke that made you smile… and then, three seconds later, that small "meh" that makes you reach for the next one.

**[VISUAL: A face right after a laugh. The smile fades. The eyes drop. The thumb moves.]**

You've felt that a thousand times. You just never had a name for it.

`(beat)`

Now — here's where it turns. (slow) Do that once, and the see-saw settles back to level. No harm done. (beat) But do it a thousand times in one evening. Every evening. For months.

**[VISUAL: The see-saw rocking faster and faster — then it gets stuck, tilted toward pain.]**

Your brain stops bouncing back to the middle. (quiet) The pain side gets heavier. And your *normal* — the way you feel when you're doing nothing at all — slowly sinks below zero.

Lembke calls this a *dopamine deficit*. (beat) Basically, you've borrowed so much good feeling from the future that you're now in debt.

**[VISUAL: Two simple lines. "Healthy normal" — flat and level. "Dopamine deficit" — drifting downward.]**

And once you're in that debt, (slow) the whole reason you scroll *changes*.

`(pause)`

You're not scrolling because it's good anymore. (firm) It stopped being good a long time ago. You're scrolling because *stopping feels worse*.

**[VISUAL: Two doors. One says "Keep scrolling — numb." One says "Put it down — feels worse right now." A hand reaches for the first.]**

Think about what that means. (beat) The boredom you feel while scrolling is real. But it's not the worst thing in the room. (slow) The worst thing is putting the phone down — and sitting in that empty, restless dip you've been running from all night.

`(beat)`

So you pick the phone. Every time. (quiet) And every post you scroll just digs the hole a little deeper for next time.

**[VISUAL: A slow spiral, turning inward and down.]**

`(pause)`

That's your answer. (warm) You're not enjoying it because you were never *supposed* to. You're using it to escape a dip that the last forty posts created.

That's not weakness. (firm) That's a chemical loop — with a billion-dollar industry quietly turning the dials.

---

### [7:30 – 9:45] — HOW TO BREAK THE LOOP

So what do you actually *do*?

**[VISUAL: Title — "THE INTERRUPT PROTOCOL" in gold serif. Four empty numbered slots underneath.]**

Good news first. (warm) You don't need a thirty-day detox. You don't need to throw your phone in a river and move to the woods. (beat) You need four small things. Done in order. Over a couple of weeks.

`(beat)`

**[VISUAL: Slot 1 fills in. The word "FRICTION" appears.]**

**Number one. Add friction.**

Right now, scrolling costs you nothing. The phone's in your hand. The app's right there. It opens in less than a second. (beat) Every one of those is a *choice* someone made — and you can undo every one.

**[VISUAL: A phone home screen. The worst app gets dragged off into a folder called "Time." Its notification dot fades to grey.]**

Take your worst app — the one you open without even thinking — and move it. Off the home screen. Into a folder, three taps deep. (beat) Even better: delete the app, and only use it in your web browser, where it's slower and clunkier and just… annoying enough.

The friction is tiny. (slow) The difference is not.

`(beat)`

**[VISUAL: Slot 2 fills in.]**

**Number two. The thirty-second check.**

When you catch yourself scrolling, don't try to *stop*. (beat) That fails. Instead, just pause. Thirty seconds. And ask yourself one honest question:

**[VISUAL: A still phone screen. A small timer ticks down from 30. The thumb is frozen.]**

*"Am I actually enjoying this?"*

`(pause)`

Not "is it useful." Not "might it be useful later." (slow) Just — am I enjoying this, right now? (beat) Most of us can't lie to ourselves for a full thirty seconds. And the honest answer usually ends it.

`(beat)`

**[VISUAL: Slot 3 fills in.]**

**Number three. Ride out the dip.**

Here's the one nobody warns you about. (firm) The first week of less scrolling will feel *worse*. Not better. *Worse*.

**[VISUAL: A line graph. It dips down for the first several days… then climbs up, higher than where it started.]**

Restless evenings. A nagging feeling that something's missing. (beat) That's just the see-saw rebalancing. That's the pain side coming back up to level.

And this is the moment most people quit — (slow) because they think the bad feeling means they *need* the phone. (warm) But it's the opposite. The bad feeling is proof the phone was the problem. (beat) Wait it out. Around day seven to ten, it lifts. And real things start to feel good again.

`(beat)`

**[VISUAL: Slot 4 fills in.]**

**Number four. Swap the slot machine for a sure thing.**

The feed is addictive because it's *unpredictable*. (beat) So in the moments you'd normally scroll — the ten minutes after dinner, the queue at the shop, the last bit before sleep — give yourself something with a small, *guaranteed* reward instead.

**[VISUAL: A phone icon gets swapped out — for a book, a walk, a cup of tea, a window to look out of.]**

A chapter of a book. A short walk. A real conversation. (beat) Honestly — even just sitting and looking out a window.

These won't feel as exciting at first. (slow) They're not built to grab you. But here's the difference — (warm) they don't leave you in debt. The good feeling is smaller… but it's clean. And after a couple of weeks, your brain starts to *prefer* them.

**[VISUAL: All four slots now full. A calm, complete checklist.]**

---

### [9:45 – 10:45] — THE PART THAT ACTUALLY MATTERS

`(quiet, slow — this is the heart of the video)`

Before you go, there's one more thing. (beat) And honestly, it might be the most important part.

**[VISUAL: Slow pull back. The phone is face-down on a table. The room is quiet and still.]**

The worst part of all this isn't the time you lose. (beat) It's the *shame*.

People feel broken. (beat) They feel weak. They remember when they could sit and read a whole book, and now they can't get through one page, and they think — (slow) "something must be wrong with me."

**[VISUAL: The face from the very beginning — but now the eyes are *up*. Off the phone. Looking out.]**

`(warm)`

There's nothing wrong with you.

`(pause)`

You are up against decades of research, billions of dollars, and some of the smartest engineers alive — (beat) all pointed at one single goal. Keep your thumb moving.

The fact that it *worked* on you doesn't mean you're weak. (firm) It means the machine did its job. That's all.

`(beat)`

And here's what changes once you actually understand the mechanism. (slow) The shame becomes useless. It was never helping anyway. (beat) And when the shame goes, what's left isn't a fight with yourself.

It's just a choice. (warm) Four small moves. Friction. The thirty-second check. Riding out the dip. Swapping the loop for something real.

**[VISUAL: The four steps appear one more time, calm and clear.]**

That's it. (beat) No detox. No willpower battle. Just enough understanding that the next time you catch yourself scrolling something you don't even like… (slow) you can put the phone down. And it doesn't have to feel like a failure.

It's just you, stepping out of a loop. (quiet) That's all it ever was.

# EXTRAS (for editing & posting — not spoken)

## Voiceover recording tips (since you're voicing this yourself)
- **Record in a soft room.** A cupboard with clothes, or under a thick blanket, beats an empty echoey room. Soft stuff kills echo.
- **Mic distance:** about a fist's width from your mouth, slightly off to the side (so "p" and "b" sounds don't pop).
- **Water nearby**, room temperature. Dry mouth = clicky sounds.
- **Record in chunks** — one section at a time. If you fluff a line, pause, clap once (gives you a visual spike to find in editing), and say the line again. Don't restart the whole thing.
- **Do two takes of the hook and the ending.** Those two matter most. Pick the best later.
- **Smile slightly on the warm bits.** People can hear a smile. It's real.
- **Leave a second of silence** at the start and end of each recording — gives the editor clean room tone.

## Word count / timing
- ~1,750 spoken words. At your calm pace (with the pauses marked), this lands around **11–12 minutes** — right in the target.
- If it runs long, the easiest safe trims are inside Mechanism One (the slot-machine recap) and the recording tips don't get spoken anyway.
