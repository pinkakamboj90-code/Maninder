### [0:00 – 0:35] HOOK

**[VISUAL: Black screen. White serif text fades in: "Your attention span isn't broken."]**

Your attention span isn't broken.

It's deconditioned.

**[VISUAL: A flexed bicep icon morphs into a brain icon, then into a small flame.]**

That's a different word. A broken thing has to be replaced. A deconditioned thing has to be trained. And that's good news — because attention behaves a lot more like a muscle than people realise. It can be lost, and it can be rebuilt. The protocol is unglamorous. It works anyway.

**[VISUAL: Title card — "HOW TO FIX YOUR ATTENTION SPAN" in serif gold on navy. Hold 1 second.]**

In the next ten minutes, I'm going to walk you through seven steps. Each one targets a specific mechanism in your brain — dopamine baseline, prefrontal load, working memory, recovery. You don't have to do them all on day one. But if you stack them across two weeks, you'll feel the change before you understand it.

---

### [0:35 – 1:30] WHY THE OLD ADVICE FAILS

Most advice on this topic is useless because it treats focus as a willpower problem.

**[VISUAL: A worn out "JUST FOCUS" poster being torn off a wall.]**

"Just put your phone away." "Just be more disciplined." "Just meditate."

That advice fails because it ignores what's actually happening in the brain. You don't have a discipline problem. You have a **conditioning** problem. Your dopamine system has spent years adapting to short, unpredictable bursts of novelty. Quiet work has stopped registering as worth your attention — not because it isn't, but because the baseline has shifted.

**[VISUAL: Two wave graphs labelled "Old baseline" (low, calm) and "New baseline" (jagged, hungry).]**

Fixing it means lowering that baseline, then progressively retraining the brain to find reward in slower signals. Like turning down a stereo so you can hear someone whisper.

**[VISUAL: A volume knob turning down. Faint whisper appears as a soundwave underneath.]**

That's what the seven steps do. Each one targets a different lever.

---

### [1:30 – 3:00] STEP 1 — RESET THE DOPAMINE BASELINE (48 HOURS)

**[VISUAL: "STEP 01 — DOPAMINE RESET" in gold serif.]**

Step one: a forty-eight hour low-stimulation window.

This is not a "dopamine detox" in the influencer sense. You're not purging a chemical. Dopamine is essential, and you make it constantly. What you're doing is removing the **artificial spikes** for long enough that your receptors recalibrate.

**[VISUAL: Receptor diagram — receptors going from "downregulated" (sparse) to "upregulated" (dense) over time.]**

For two days, cut the things that hit your reward system in short bursts: social media feeds, short-form video, news scrolling, online shopping, gambling-style apps, and — if you can — alcohol. Keep the things that require sustained engagement: a long film, a book, a walk, a conversation, work that takes longer than ten minutes to feel rewarding.

You'll feel restless on day one. Bored on day two. By the end of forty-eight hours, something quieter starts to happen — small things become interesting again. A coffee tastes better. A page reads faster. That's not poetry. That's your reward system finding signal in lower-amplitude inputs.

**[VISUAL: Graph showing reward sensitivity dipping on day 1, climbing on day 2, flattening higher on day 3.]**

Forty-eight hours is the floor, not the ceiling. Some people need a week. Don't skip this step. It's the foundation everything else sits on.

---

### [3:00 – 4:15] STEP 2 — REMOVE THE PHONE FROM YOUR FIELD OF VIEW

**[VISUAL: "STEP 02 — OUT OF SIGHT" in gold serif.]**

Step two: when you're working, your phone goes in another room.

Not face-down. Not in a drawer at the desk. Not in a pocket. **In another room.**

**[VISUAL: Three setups appear — desk, drawer, another room — performance bars climbing accordingly.]**

In 2017, a researcher at the University of Texas named Adrian Ward ran a study with around eight hundred participants. They were given tasks that measured working memory and fluid intelligence. The phone-in-another-room group performed measurably better than the phone-on-desk group — even when the phone was off. Even when nobody touched it.

**[VISUAL: Citation card — "Ward et al., 2017 — Journal of the Association for Consumer Research."]**

He called the effect **brain drain**. The cost of keeping a phone in view isn't the time you spend on it. It's the cognitive bandwidth your brain spends *not* picking it up. That bandwidth is exactly the bandwidth you need for deep work.

**[VISUAL: A working memory meter draining slowly while a phone glows nearby.]**

There's no clever workaround. No app. No trick. Out of the room is the only setting that buys back the bandwidth.

---

### [4:15 – 5:30] STEP 3 — TRAIN ATTENTION IN PROGRESSIVE REPS

**[VISUAL: "STEP 03 — REPS, NOT MARATHONS" in gold serif.]**

Step three: train attention the way you'd train a muscle. Short reps first. Build up.

Most people fail because they sit down and try to focus for two hours after months of fragmentation. The brain refuses. They feel terrible, conclude they're broken, and quit. The mistake is the dose, not the person.

**[VISUAL: A weight rack — small dumbbell first, scaling up to heavier ones. Each weight labelled in minutes.]**

Start with a single twenty-five-minute block. Phone in another room. One tab. One task. Set a timer. When the timer goes, stop — even if you're in flow. The point is to teach your brain that focus has a clear start and a clear end.

Do that once a day for three days. Then go to thirty minutes. Then forty-five. Then two ninety-minute blocks across the day. By the end of two weeks, you've built the same kind of progression a runner uses to go from one mile to ten — without injury.

**[VISUAL: A two-week calendar — blocks of focus time growing in length and number across the days.]**

Every time your mind wanders during a block, gently bring it back. Don't punish yourself. The bringing-back **is** the rep. That's the actual exercise — not the staying-still. The neural circuit you're strengthening is the one that notices drift and returns.

---

### [5:30 – 6:45] STEP 4 — DELIBERATE BOREDOM (10 MINUTES A DAY)

**[VISUAL: "STEP 04 — BOREDOM ON PURPOSE" in gold serif.]**

Step four: ten minutes of deliberate boredom, every day.

This sounds absurd. It is the most important step.

**[VISUAL: A person sitting on a chair in an empty room. Slow zoom-in. No music.]**

Sit. Don't scroll. Don't read. Don't listen to anything. Don't journal. Stare at a wall, look out a window, or close your eyes. Ten minutes. That's it.

The reason this matters is that boredom isn't the absence of stimulation — it's the discomfort that comes when your brain reaches for novelty and doesn't find it. That discomfort is exactly the signal you need to retrain. Every minute you sit through it, you're teaching your nervous system that low-stimulation states aren't an emergency.

**[VISUAL: A discomfort curve — peaks early, drops sharply by minute six, flattens calm by minute ten.]**

There's also a quieter benefit. Neuroscientists have shown that the brain's **default mode network** — the circuit that activates when you're not focused on anything external — is where memory consolidation, planning, and creative connection happen. Constant input shuts it off. Boredom turns it back on.

**[VISUAL: Brain diagram — default mode network lights up softly when external inputs go dark.]**

If ten minutes feels impossible at first, start with three. Build up. The goal is tolerance, not enlightenment.

---

### [6:45 – 8:00] STEP 5 — REBUILD WORKING MEMORY WITH PAPER READING

**[VISUAL: "STEP 05 — READ ON PAPER" in gold serif.]**

Step five: read on paper. Twenty minutes a day. Anything you actually want to read.

This isn't a romantic preference. It's a structural one.

**[VISUAL: Side-by-side scroll of digital text vs a printed page — the digital one fragments and jumps, the page sits still.]**

Reading on a screen is fundamentally different from reading on paper. Screens are designed to be scanned. They're full of competing visual elements, link colours, scroll cues, and — for most of us — an underlying expectation that something else might pop up at any moment. Your brain reads them in **F-pattern** scans, taking in surfaces.

Paper has none of that. Paper forces linear, sustained attention. Studies on reading comprehension have repeatedly found higher recall and deeper inference when the same text is read on paper compared to a screen. The difference compounds for longer or more complex material.

**[VISUAL: Bar chart — comprehension and recall higher for paper across multiple study citations.]**

Twenty minutes a day, one book, on paper. Phone in another room. No multitasking. You're rebuilding the cognitive scaffolding for sustained narrative attention — the exact scaffolding that short video has eroded.

If you don't read books, read printed long-form essays. The format is the medicine.

---

### [8:00 – 9:00] STEP 6 — THE PHYSICAL INFRASTRUCTURE (SLEEP, CARDIO, BREATH)

**[VISUAL: "STEP 06 — THE PHYSICAL LAYER" in gold serif.]**

Step six: the physical infrastructure your brain runs on.

You can do every cognitive step in this video and still fail at attention if the hardware isn't supported. Three things matter most.

**[VISUAL: Three icons appear — bed, running figure, lungs.]**

**Sleep.** Seven to nine hours. Deep work isn't possible on six. Your prefrontal cortex is the first system to degrade under sleep debt — judgement, suppression of impulses, working memory, emotional regulation. All of them are attention's quiet partners. Without them, you're trying to run software on a machine that hasn't been rebooted.

**Cardio.** Twenty to thirty minutes of zone-two cardio — a brisk walk, a slow jog — three to four times a week. The evidence here is unusually strong: aerobic activity increases brain-derived neurotrophic factor, improves cerebral blood flow, and measurably improves sustained attention in adults. It also lowers baseline stress, which is one of the biggest hidden saboteurs of focus.

**Nasal breathing.** During focus blocks, breathe through your nose, not your mouth. Slow, quiet, in through the nose, out through the nose. It sounds trivial. It isn't — nasal breathing produces nitric oxide, regulates the autonomic nervous system, and keeps you in a state where the prefrontal cortex stays online instead of being hijacked by stress signalling.

**[VISUAL: Three small graphs — sleep, cardio, breath — each lifting a "focus capacity" line.]**

The hardware first. Then the software.

---

### [9:00 – 10:00] STEP 7 — THE WEEKLY AUDIT

**[VISUAL: "STEP 07 — THE AUDIT" in gold serif.]**

Step seven: every Sunday, ten minutes, an honest audit.

**[VISUAL: A simple journal page being filled in. Three columns: "What pulled my attention," "What I finished," "What's next."]**

Three questions:

One — *what pulled my attention this week?* Apps, conversations, recurring thoughts, anxieties. Don't moralise. Just notice. Patterns become obvious in writing that are invisible in your head.

Two — *what did I finish that I wouldn't have a month ago?* This is your evidence. The whole protocol is anchored to proof, not feeling. You will have days that feel like nothing changed. The audit shows you what actually did.

Three — *which step needs more work next week?* Pick one. Not three. One. The protocol works because it's progressive, not all-or-nothing.

**[VISUAL: A checklist forming — Step 1 → Step 7 — with one item circled for emphasis.]**

The audit is what turns the protocol from a fourteen-day reset into a sustainable system. Most people do something for two weeks, feel better, drift back, and conclude it didn't work. The audit prevents the drift. It's cheap, it's quiet, and it's the difference between a phase and a baseline.

---

### [10:00 – 10:45] THE BIGGER POINT

Here's the part nobody says clearly enough.

**[VISUAL: A wide pull-back. The seven steps sit on screen as a clean stack.]**

Your attention is the most valuable thing you own. Not your time — anyone with eight hours can waste them. **Attention** is what turns time into work, work into output, output into meaning. Every system around you — every app, every feed, every notification — has been engineered to take small slices of it and convert them into someone else's revenue.

This protocol isn't about productivity culture. It isn't about becoming a monk. It's about reclaiming the parts of your day where focus actually matters — and putting fences around them.

**[VISUAL: The seven steps animate into a fenced-in garden — a small calm space inside a noisy city.]**

Reset the baseline. Remove the phone from your field of view. Train in reps. Tolerate boredom. Read on paper. Support the hardware. Audit weekly.

Seven steps. Layered, not stacked all at once. Two weeks to feel the shift. A few months to make it your default.

## STRUCTURE LOGIC (Why this script works)

**1. Reframe in the first 10 seconds.** The viewer arrives believing their attention is broken. The script swaps "broken" for "deconditioned" before the title card. That single word change reframes the entire video from confession to training plan.

**2. Each step targets a distinct mechanism.**
- Step 1 → Dopamine receptor recalibration
- Step 2 → Working memory load (brain drain)
- Step 3 → Progressive overload (neuroplasticity)
- Step 4 → Default mode network restoration
- Step 5 → Sustained narrative attention scaffolding
- Step 6 → Physical substrate (sleep, BDNF, autonomic regulation)
- Step 7 → Behavioural feedback loop (audit)

If a viewer asks "why does this step exist?" each one has a clean answer.

**3. The order is non-arbitrary.** Reset → remove friction → train → tolerate stillness → rebuild scaffolding → support hardware → audit. You can't train (Step 3) before resetting baseline (Step 1). You can't audit (Step 7) before there's something to audit.

**4. The "rep" framing is the hero metaphor.** Most attention content uses the phrase "willpower." This script replaces it with "rep." Reps are forgivable. Reps are progressive. Reps don't require character — they require count.

**5. The CTA sets up the next video.** Episode 5 (afternoon attention slump) is teased without begging. The "same architecture, different lever" framing makes the channel feel like a series, not a stream.
