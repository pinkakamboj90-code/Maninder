### [0:25 – 1:30] THE PROBLEM (Why this hits home)

Here's what I want you to notice.

**[VISUAL: Slow-motion close-up of a hand reaching for a phone on a desk.]**

Right now — as you're watching this — there's a small voice in your head suggesting you check something. A notification. A text. The time. Anything. You've been here for twenty-five seconds. The voice is already there.

**[VISUAL: Subtle red glow pulses on a phone in the background.]**

That voice isn't weakness. It's a chemical reflex. Your brain has been trained — by years of small, intermittent rewards — to expect a hit of novelty every minute or two. When it doesn't get one, it gets restless. Like a smoker reaching for a pack that isn't there.

This is what I'd call **the focus tax**. And almost nobody talks about it because it's invisible. You don't notice the cost — you notice the *absence* of focus. The half-finished projects. The tabs you've had open for three weeks. The book on your nightstand you've been "almost finished with" since March.

**[VISUAL: Quick cuts — open browser tabs, a dusty bookmark, a half-eaten meal next to a phone.]**

### [1:30 – 4:00] THE MECHANISM (What's actually broken)

Let me explain what's going on. Three things — all happening at the same time.

**[VISUAL: "THREE FORCES" appears in gold serif. Three numbered icons fade in.]**

**One: your brain has been rewired by reward schedules.**

Every notification, every "like," every refresh of a feed — it triggers a small release of dopamine. Not from the reward itself, but from the *anticipation* of one. This is called **variable ratio reinforcement** — the same psychology used in slot machines.

**[VISUAL: Slot machine animation — pulls down, dopamine molecule fires in brain icon.]**

The cruelty of it is this: a guaranteed reward is far less addictive than an unpredictable one. Your phone isn't addictive because it always gives you something good. It's addictive because *sometimes* it does. Your brain learns to keep checking, just in case.

After enough repetitions — and we're talking millions, across years — your dopamine system gets used to that level of stimulation. Quiet, deep work no longer registers as rewarding. So you reach for the phone again. Not because you want to. Because your baseline has shifted.

**[VISUAL: Two dopamine baselines — "Old normal" (low, steady) vs "New normal" (jagged, hungry).]**

**Two: just having your phone near you is draining your brain.**

In 2017, a researcher at the University of Texas — Adrian Ward — ran a study with about 800 people. He gave them tasks that measured working memory and problem-solving. Some had their phones on the desk, face-down. Some had them in a pocket. Some had them in another room.

**[VISUAL: Three setups appear — phone on desk, phone in pocket, phone in another room. Performance bars rise differently.]**

The group with phones in another room performed measurably better. Not just slightly. Significantly. Even when the phone was off. Even when nobody touched it. Just being in the room with your phone is using up cognitive resources — your brain has to spend energy *not* thinking about it.

He called this **brain drain**.

**[VISUAL: Citation card — "Ward et al., 2017 — Journal of the Association for Consumer Research."]**

**Three: every interruption costs you twenty-three minutes.**

This one comes from Gloria Mark — the same researcher who measured the 47-second attention span. In separate work, she found that after an interruption, it takes an average of twenty-three minutes and fifteen seconds to fully return to the original task.

**[VISUAL: Stopwatch animation — "23:15" appears in large text.]**

Most people get interrupted — by a phone, a Slack ping, an email, a thought — every few minutes. So they're never actually focused. They're permanently in the warm-up phase. Always *about* to start. Never *in* the work.

**[VISUAL: A graph showing a person's "focus depth" — never reaching the deep zone, constantly resetting.]**

Now stack those three together: rewired reward system, brain drain from your phone's presence, twenty-three-minute cost per interruption. That's why you can't focus. Not because you're lazy. Not because you're flawed. Because the environment you live in is engineered, hour by hour, to fragment you.

### [4:00 – 7:30] THE 7-DAY FIX

So how do we get out of this? Here's the protocol. Seven days. One change per day. Layered, not all at once. Most people fail because they try to do everything on day one — feel terrible — and quit by Wednesday.

**[VISUAL: "THE 7-DAY PROTOCOL" title card. Gold serif on navy.]**

**Day 1: Phone in another room. Two hours.**

That's it. One block, two hours, phone out of sight. Not in your pocket. Not face-down. *In another room.* If you live in one room, put it in a drawer. The point is breaking the visual cue — the brain needs to stop scanning for it.

You'll feel anxious in the first thirty minutes. That's normal. That feeling is your dopamine system asking for its hit. Let it pass. It does, every time, around minute forty.

**[VISUAL: A small clock — anxiety peak at 30 min, drops at 40 min.]**

**Day 2: Add a notification purge.**

Open your settings. Turn off every push notification except calls from a small list of people. Not "important" notifications. Not "the ones I need." All of them. Email, social, news, shopping — silenced.

You can still check things. You just have to choose to check them. The difference is enormous: choice vs. reaction.

**[VISUAL: Notification panel emptying out — silenced icons stack up.]**

**Day 3: Single-tab work.**

For one work session, allow only one browser tab open. One. If you need a different page, close the current one first. This sounds petty. It isn't. Visible tabs are open loops your brain has to track. Tabs equals tax.

**[VISUAL: 30 tabs collapsing down to 1.]**

**Day 4: The morning hour.**

Don't touch your phone for the first sixty minutes after waking up. Not to check the weather. Not to read the news. Not to scroll. The first hour of the day sets the dopamine baseline for everything that follows. If you start in reactive mode, you stay in reactive mode.

**[VISUAL: A clock — first hour highlighted in gold; phone icon greyed out beside it.]**

**Day 5: Boredom on purpose.**

For ten minutes today, do nothing. Sit. Stare at a wall. Don't scroll. Don't listen to a podcast. Don't read.

This sounds insane. It's the most important step. Your brain has lost the ability to tolerate stillness — and stillness is where focus rebuilds itself. Boredom isn't the enemy of attention. Boredom is the *fertiliser*.

**[VISUAL: A person sitting still on a chair. Empty room. Slow zoom in.]**

**Day 6: One deep block.**

Today, schedule a ninety-minute block for one task. Phone in another room. One tab. Notifications off. No music with lyrics. Just you and the work.

You won't be perfect. Your brain will try to escape. Let it try, and bring it back. Every time you bring it back, you're doing a rep. That's the actual exercise.

**[VISUAL: Brain doing "reps" — like a dumbbell curl. Each interruption recovered = one rep.]**

**Day 7: The audit.**

Take ten minutes today and just notice. How does focus feel different? What did you finish that you wouldn't have finished a week ago? What feels easier? What still pulls at you?

You're not aiming for a permanent transformation in seven days. You're aiming for **proof** — proof that the system works on your brain. Once you have proof, the protocol becomes a tool you can pick up whenever you've drifted.

**[VISUAL: A reflective journal page being written — checkboxes filling in.]**

### [7:30 – 8:45] THE BIGGER POINT

Here's what nobody tells you about focus.

It's not a personality trait. It's not something you have or don't have. It's not destroyed by smartphones in a way you can't recover from. The same scientists who measured the 47-second attention span also measured what happens when you remove the noise: focus comes back. Within days, not years.

**[VISUAL: A wave graph showing attention rebuilding — slow, then steeper, then strong.]**

You weren't born to be a worse version of yourself than you were five years ago. You haven't lost anything permanent. You've just been living inside a machine that's optimised — relentlessly, by people far smarter than the average user — to take small slices of your attention and turn them into money.

The fix isn't to be a monk. It isn't to throw your phone in a lake. It's to take back the parts of your day where focus actually matters — and put fences around them.

Phone in another room. Notifications off. One tab. Quiet morning. Tolerated boredom. Deep block. Honest audit.

Seven days. One change a day. That's it.

## WHY THIS SCRIPT IS DIFFERENT (My Notes)

Most scripts on this topic fail in three ways. Here's what I did differently.

**1. I didn't lecture from the start.**
Bad scripts open with: *"In this video, I'll explain..."* This script opens with a stat that hits the viewer before they can defend against it. Then immediately removes guilt: "It's not your fault."

**2. I made the science feel like *us*, not *them*.**
Most science videos quote studies in passive voice. ("It has been shown that...") This script names the researcher (Gloria Mark, Adrian Ward), gives them a face, says when and where. That's how you make 70-year-old psychology feel like investigative journalism.

**3. I gave one task per day, not seven.**
The biggest viewer drop-off in self-help videos is when the "solution" turns into a 14-step protocol. This one does seven steps but layered — one per day — so it feels possible, not punishing.

**4. The personal "voice" doesn't lie.**
Notice I never invented a fake personal story (e.g., "I used to be addicted to my phone..."). I used **observational empathy** instead ("If you've felt like your brain is broken — you're not imagining it"). This works just as well, sounds genuine, and doesn't compromise integrity.

**5. The CTA isn't begging.**
"If this gave you something useful, subscribe." That's it. No "PLEASE LIKE AND SUBSCRIBE." No bell-ringing. The viewer who got value will subscribe. The one who didn't won't be moved by pleading.
